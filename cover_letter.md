# Cover letter — draft

**To:** The Editors, *Nature Energy*
**Re:** Submission of an Article for consideration

---

Dear Editors,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, **"Energy sovereignty leakage: gas import substitution could relocate global emissions to mid-century,"** for consideration as an Article in *Nature Energy*.

Reducing dependence on a dominant foreign gas supplier — energy sovereignty — has become an explicit policy goal across the world, from Mexico's drive to replace United States pipeline imports with domestic shale to Europe's retreat from Russian gas. The premise, almost always implicit, is that cutting imports reduces both strategic dependence and global gas use, and is therefore good for the climate. Our work shows that this territorial premise can be exactly wrong, and identifies the precise condition under which it fails.

Using a calibrated 19-region global gas-trade model, we introduce and quantify **Energy Sovereignty Leakage (ESL)** — the fraction of a sovereignty-driven import cut that reappears as gas consumption elsewhere. Our central findings are:

1. **On today's economics, sovereignty substitution is climate-benign.** The gas Mexico stops importing is largely left unproduced; global gas use genuinely falls (ESL ≲ 2%).

2. **Leakage is a threshold phenomenon, not a fixed quantity.** It switches on only once delivered US LNG is driven below the price of the Asian coal it would displace — and two realistic, policy-driven forces push it across that wall: a security premium for reliable US supply, and a carbon price on coal.

3. **The instruments meant to advance the transition are what unlock the leak, and the climate sign is methane-contingent.** Because a carbon price charges coal combustion but leaves the upstream methane and LNG-chain emissions of the competing gas unpriced, it can convert a security-motivated gas policy into a net source of warming — a mechanism in the lineage of the green paradox, here driven by *asymmetric* rather than anticipated pricing. Whether relocation helps or harms the climate turns on one external parameter: by our lifecycle assessment, shale-LNG is marginally cleaner than coal at central methane but becomes dirtier once upstream methane exceeds ~3% on a 20-year horizon — the range that measurement increasingly supports.

A methodological contribution underpins these results: we couple the trade optimisation to a process-level lifecycle inventory (ecoinvent/Brightway) at every solved year, so emissions are resolved region-by-region on a full lifecycle basis rather than by fixed factors.

We believe this is timely and broadly significant for the *Nature Energy* readership. It connects two literatures usually kept apart — the geopolitics of energy security and the climate accounting of gas — and delivers a concrete, policy-actionable conclusion: import-substitution and carbon-pricing policies must be paired with lifecycle-complete pricing or supply-side conditions, or they will relocate emissions rather than reduce them. The Mexico–US case is the sharpest current instance, but the mechanism generalises to any sovereignty policy that displaces a swing exporter.

We are candid about the model's scope: it establishes the *threshold* for leakage robustly, while the above-threshold *magnitude* is reported as a conditional range rather than a single point estimate, and we discuss the framework extension that would resolve it. All data are public and the full model and analysis code are openly available.

The main text is approximately 3,000 words (excluding abstract, Methods, references and figure legends), with 7 main-text display items, 2 Extended Data figures, and Supplementary Information. It has not been published elsewhere and is not under consideration by another journal. We have no competing interests to declare.

We would be grateful for your consideration and look forward to your response.
